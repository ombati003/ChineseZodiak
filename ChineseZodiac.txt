The Chinese Zodiac: Origin and the Chinese Horoscope

The Chinese zodiac is a system that assigns an animal to each year in a repeating 12-year cycle. Unlike the Western zodiac, which is built around months and constellations, the Chinese zodiac changes its sign every year, following the Chinese lunar calendar. In Chinese it is called Sheng Xiao, which roughly means "birth likeness," and it is tied to the twelve earthly branches, an older Chinese method of organizing years and hours in sets of twelve. The twelve animals, in order, are the Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog and Pig.

The true origin of the zodiac is unclear. Some accounts trace it to the Warring States period, or to the Qin and Han dynasties, but historians have not settled the question. The most popular explanation is a legend in which the Jade Emperor invited all the animals to a race across a river, and the first twelve to finish earned a place in the calendar in the order they arrived. In the story, the Rat won by riding on the Ox and jumping ahead at the last moment. Other legends say the animals were summoned to a banquet by the Buddha instead. Neighboring countries also adopted the system, sometimes changing an animal. In Vietnam, for example, the cat takes the place of the rabbit.

The idea behind the Chinese horoscope is that the animal of a person's birth year shapes their personality. For instance, the Rat is linked with cleverness and the Horse with leadership. In the past, the zodiac was also used alongside other forms of Chinese prediction to suggest the nature of coming harvests and political events. Today it is part of Chinese folk tradition and is especially celebrated during the Chinese New Year, and its popularity has grown in the years since the end of the Cultural Revolution.
